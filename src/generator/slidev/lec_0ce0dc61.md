<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">1. Introduction: The Crisis of Monoculture and the Definition of Bibliodiversity</h1>

- Monocultures of the mind threaten scholarly health.
- Diversity prevents vendor lock-in and monopoly risks.
- Decline driven by profit-focused multinational publishers.
- Open science offers a chance to reverse this trend.
- Jussieu Call defines our path toward bibliodiversity.
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">1.1. The Decline of Scholarly Communications Ecosystems</h1>

- Bibliodiversity has declined for decades toward a monoculture.
- Dominant publishers prioritize profit over ecosystem health.
- Multinationals create homogenized formats and high prices.
- This shift risks vendor lock-in and stifles epistemic pluralism.
- We must reverse this trend to support diverse research communities.
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">1.2. Defining Bibliodiversity and Epistemic Pluralism</h1>

- Bibliodiversity: Cultural diversity applied to books and publications.
- It is a self-sustaining system of storytelling and production.
- Producers act as ecosystem inhabitants sustaining a healthy eco-social system.
- Epistemic Pluralism: Knowledge practices must not privilege specific ways of knowing.
- We must address epistemic injustice to include diverse knowers and values.
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">2. Barriers to a Diverse Scholarly Communications System</h1>

- Dominance of English limits local knowledge use
- Concentrated infrastructures lock communities into monopolies
- Subscription models marginalize diverse open services
- Narrow journal assessment ignores qualitative impact
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">2.1. Language Dominance and Epistemic Injustice</h1>

- Dominant English position ignores local linguistic practices.
- Evaluation bias equates English publications with high quality.
- Local audiences cannot access research in different languages.
- Disqualifying local languages prevents societal use of knowledge.
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">2.2. Infrastructure Concentration and Funding Limitations</h1>

- Dominant language pressures researchers to publish in English
- Concentrated infrastructure locks communities into proprietary services
- Subscription funding models marginalize diverse and innovative services
- Narrow journal assessment measures hinder true research diversity
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">3. A Multi-Stakeholder Call for Action</h1>

- We call for concerted action to reverse bibliodiversity's decline.
- Funders must endorse DORA and support local, interoperable infrastructures.
- Researchers should use open community-based systems and advocate for their value.
- Libraries need standard models to fund diverse content beyond transactional fees.
- Policy makers must align incentives with local research priorities and values.
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">3.1. Actions for Funders, Institutions, and Researchers</h1>

- Funders: Endorse DORA for fair research evaluation
- Institutions: Support local, open, interoperable infrastructures
- Researchers: Use community-based tools and advocate for them
- All: Endorse the Jussieu Call for coordinated strategies
---

<h1 style="color: #e2b96f;">3.2. Actions for Libraries, Policy Makers, and All Communities</h1>

- Libraries assess diversity and fund alternatives to transactional models
- Policy makers embed bibliodiversity in open science frameworks
- All communities endorse the Jussieu Call for Open Science and Bibliodiversity
- Develop coordinated strategies aligning local priorities with funding and infrastructures
